Growth ≠ Development

South Africa's GDP per capita (PPP) places it among Africa's wealthiest economies, yet its HDI collapses once inequality is factored in and this is one of the steepest losses in the world.[3] The Lorenz curve alongside shows just how far income distribution sits from perfect equality: a Gini coefficient near 63, the highest recorded of any country.[5][6]

Growth is happening, but it does not turn into broad based development because the benefits are concentrated, not shared.

Lorenz Curve

South Africa's GDP per capita (PPP) places it among Africa's wealthiest economies, yet its HDI (0.741) collapses to just 0.476 once inequality is factored in which is a 36% loss and is among the steepest in the world. Lesson 3 · Syllabus 4.10

Strategy & the Exchange Rate

Since 1994, South Africa has run a mixed strategy: market liberalisation alongside interventionist redistribution.

STRENGTHS
  • Trade liberalisation & WTO entry (1995) drew FDI into autos, mining & finance[10]
  • Social grants are among the most redistributive in the world. fiscal transfers lift the effective Gini by ~20 points[11]
  • National Development Plan gives long term policy direction beyond electoral cycles[10]
LIMITATIONS
  • Black Economic Empowerment (BEE) criticised for benefiting a narrow elite rather than broad based growth[10]
  • State owned enterprises (Eskom, Transnet) are mismanaged & debt laden, (e.g load shedding deters investment)[9]
  • Structural reform (energy, logistics, labour market) has moved too slowly to lift the growth rate[10]

The Exchange Rate: Fixed vs. Floating

In reality, the rand is free floating. South Africa abandoned currency pegs in the 1990s. The rand absorbs shocks continuously (commodity prices, load shedding, political risk), which cushions export competitiveness but creates high day to day volatility.[12][13]

Why not peg the rand?
A fixed rate would offer FDI attracting stability and an inflation anchor but it requires large reserves, and a shock can force a sudden, destabilising devaluation instead of a gradual adjustment.[12]

Lesson 4 · Syllabus 4.10

The WTO Reality Check

CASE STUDY: THE SOUTH AFRICA–BRAZIL FROZEN CHICKEN DISPUTE

In 2011 the South African Poultry Association accused Brazil of dumping frozen chicken below cost, threatening thousands of local jobs. South Africa's trade regulator (ITAC) imposed provisional anti dumping duties in 2012. Brazil challenged the investigation at the WTO Dispute Settlement Body, arguing it was procedurally flawed.[14]

In March 2013 South Africa's government rejected the specific anti dumping measure, but sidestepped the ruling's spirit by raising a general tariff on all countries without preferential trade deals to as high as 82%, achieving the same protective outcome by another route.[15]

The pattern repeated: fresh anti dumping duties followed against the EU (2015) and a safeguard tariff (2018), before duties against Brazil and four EU states were suspended in 2022 to ease food price pressure on consumers below the poverty line.[16][17]

WHERE THE WTO FALLS SHORT

The WTO can rule on procedure, but it cannot force a developing country to give up protecting a vulnerable industry — nor stop a wealthier trade bloc from relitigating the same dispute for over a decade. Rules apply equally on paper; capacity to negotiate and absorb retaliation does not.[14][15]

Reading the diagram, step by step

Before the tariff: South Africa is a price taker on the world chicken market, so the world price Pw sets the domestic price. At that price, South African farmers supply Q1 and South African households demand Q2. Since Q2 is greater than Q1, the gap between them is met by imports.

ITAC imposes the tariff: the price paid in South Africa rises from Pw to Pw plus the tariff. Two things happen at once. Domestic supply expands along the Sd curve from Q1 to Q3, because local farmers can now profitably produce more. Domestic demand contracts along the Dd curve from Q2 to Q4, because chicken is now more expensive for households. Imports shrink to the gap between Q3 and Q4.

Who wins and who loses: producers gain area 1, and the government collects area 3 as tariff revenue. Consumers lose all four areas combined, since they now pay more for less chicken. Areas 2 and 4 are deadweight loss: welfare that simply disappears, gained by nobody. That net loss (areas 2 and 4) is the economic cost of protecting 110,000 poultry jobs, and it is exactly why the WTO and free trade economists object to tariffs even when the domestic industry argument feels compelling.

Lesson 5 · 2-Minute Interactive Moment

Policy Dilemma: Farmers, or the Consumer?

THE CROSSROAD

It's 2012. Brazilian frozen chicken is undercutting local farmers. 110,000+ poultry jobs are on the line, but tariffs mean more expensive protein for households already below the poverty line. What should South Africa do?

What actually happened

South Africa protected the industry — general tariffs up to 82% on countries without preferential trade deals.[15] But in 2022, those duties were suspended for a year when food inflation hit consumers hardest.[16] Neither choice stayed permanent — it's a tradeoff, not a formula.

Key takeaway

Growth is not development, and every real development decision, from tariffs to exchange rates, is a tradeoff, not a guaranteed win.
